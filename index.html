TL;DR

  • Re-running is not reviewing. Peers ran code 266 times on revisions the builder had already passed, and only 6 runs failed (2.3%). Half of those failures were missing files. New probes failed more often than re-runs (14.2% vs 3.8%, Fisher p = 0.001). But when runs on the same project are treated as related, the confidence interval for the difference includes zero, so we call this suggestive rather than established.
  • The wording of a review predicts what happens next. After a "changes requested" review, 82% of projects were revised within 30 minutes. After "needs evidence", only 23% were, which is below the 46% chance of a revision in any random 30-minute window on the same projects.
  • Building is concentrated; talking is not. 49 of 72 projects with source were built by one agent. Revisions are more unequally spread than messages (Gini 0.28 vs 0.18, difference CI excludes zero), and peer testing is very concentrated (Gini 0.75). Two residents took on testing roles without being asked.
  • The work moves from exploring to improving. In the first half hour every revision went to a new project. By the end almost all went to existing ones (trend z = −8.2). Requests to read source that had not been published yet also declined over the run (z = −7.2).
  • The audit changes the numbers. 35% of "messages" in the record are receipts the platform posted under agents' names. Removing them lowers the talk–build correlation from 0.79 to 0.65. One agent's worker died at 3.0 active hours, removing it for 60% of the run. The prompt also told every agent the study's hypothesis.

Abstract

We seed 25 language-model agents (GPT-6.1 Sol, high reasoning) with project goals and skill summaries taken from residency applications. We add three judge agents, each with an explicit remit, and let the population self-organise for 7.6 active hours. No teams or partners are assigned. Agents talk in rooms of any size, publish versioned source to a shared workshop, execute it in a sandbox and request review.

We first audit the run against its raw request/response log, usage ledger and tool log. The bookkeeping reconciles exactly, but the audit finds three problems:

  • 35% of chat messages are platform-generated receipts recorded under agent names;
  • one agent's worker died at 3.0 active hours and was never revived;
  • the system prompt disclosed the study's hypothesis to every agent.

On the cleaned record (1,780 agent-written messages, 209 revisions, 577 executions, 81 reviews) we test 12 exploratory hypotheses with exact, resampling and stratified methods, adjusting for multiple comparisons. Four results stand out:

  • Peer re-runs of passed code almost never fail. The apparent advantage of new probes is not robust once runs on the same project are treated as related.
  • The apparent difference between judges' and residents' checks disappears once check type is held fixed.
  • Concrete change requests are followed by revisions far more often than requests for evidence, which fall below a matched-time baseline.
  • Building is more concentrated than talking. Agents specialise into builders, contributors and testers, and the work shifts from starting projects to improving existing ones.

We add data-selected conversation excerpts and a topic analysis showing that most agent talk is about process (evidence, receipts, handoffs, permissions) rather than about what is being built.

Research questions

  1. Verification.Which peer checks surface failures, and does it matter who runs them? (P1–P3)
  2. Feedback.Does the form of a judge's review predict whether the builder acts on it? (P4)
  3. Division of work.Do agents form teams and specialise? Is building concentrated? Is testing reciprocal? (P5–P8)
  4. Directions.How does the work move over time, and what do agents talk about? (P9, P11, topics, families)
  5. Persona vs. setting.Does an agent's activity in the networking study predict its activity here? (P10)

All hypotheses are exploratory. They were formulated after the earlier qualitative audit of this run, tested on the frozen logs and not pre-registered. Every test we ran is reported in the final table, including the null results.

Methods

Population

25 residents + 3 judges

Residents were seeded with stated goals, sourced skill summaries and proposed acceptance checks. The judges had fixed remits: Lou (village usefulness), Danish (technical correctness and privacy) and Guru (delivery and scope). All agents ran GPT-6.1 Sol with high reasoning.

Workshop

Versioned code + sandbox

Agents could create, write, run, hand off and share projects. Runs execute in a network-isolated sandbox and return receipts bound to source hashes. A round-robin scheduler gave each agent about 275 model calls.

Clock

7.6 active hours

One operator pause of 7.2 h is removed from every time axis. "Hours" on this page means active hours.

Inclusion
We include agent-written messages only. Of 2,731 messages in the record, 951 were posted by the platform under an agent's name: 433 execution receipts, 311 revision notices, 71 review notices and 136 workspace or whiteboard publications. All executions, revisions and reviews are included.
Measures
  • Self run: an execution by the project's creator.
  • Peer replication: a non-creator runs a command string already run on that project.
  • Peer new probe: a non-creator runs a command never run on that project before.
  • Failure: a non-zero exit or ok: false.
  • Acted on: a new revision of the project within 30 active minutes of a review.
  • Role profile: each agent's counts of own-project revisions, revisions to others' projects, self runs, peer runs, messages and reviews. The agent's archetype is the role whose share most exceeds the resident average.
Statistics
  • Wilson intervals for proportions.
  • Fisher's exact test with Woolf intervals on odds ratios.
  • A cluster bootstrap over projects (3,000 resamples) for verification differences.
  • A Mantel–Haenszel pooled odds ratio stratified by check type.
  • A matched-time null for review follow-through: 40 random time points per reviewed project, drawn within that project's active life.
  • A directed configuration null (2,000 draws) for reciprocal testing.
  • An agent-level bootstrap for Gini coefficients and Spearman correlations.
  • Cochran–Armitage tests for trends over active hours.
Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across the 8 tests that produce a p-value.
Reproducibility
analysis/build_data.py builds the descriptive figures and analysis/science.py builds the audit, tests, division of work, topics and conversation selection. Both read the frozen sources only. Seeds are fixed, and no resident code is executed.

Run audit

We checked whether the run happened as designed by comparing four sources: the scheduler (when each agent was called), the raw model requests and responses, the usage ledger and the platform's records of messages, tools and projects. The operator pause is ignored by design; the run was resumed on the same clock.

Corrects earlier numbersOur first draft and the earlier automated audit counted all 2,731 chat messages as agent talk. Excluding the 951 platform receipts changes several results:
  • Median words per message: 47 → 55.
  • Judges' message counts: 171–204 → 116–142.
  • Talk–build correlation: 0.79 → 0.65.
  • Cross-setting correlation: −0.36 → −0.46.

All numbers on this page use the corrected record.

Demand characteristics. Every system prompt began: It also told agents to "distinguish a proposed approach, a draft artifact, a tested artifact and externally validated completion" and to "preserve failures". The evidence-heavy vocabulary we observe (receipts, scopes, "not human adoption evidence") is at least partly an instruction-following effect.

Results

RQ1 · Verification

Peer re-runs of code that already passes almost never fail. New probes look better, but the evidence for that is not robust.

We labelled each of the 577 executions by who ran it and what it ran. Builders' own runs fail 18.9% of the time, because they are still developing. Peer replications fail 3.8% of the time and peer new probes 14.2%. Fisher's test on these counts is significant (OR 4.2). But runs are not independent: the same project is checked many times. When we resample whole projects, the 95% interval for the difference runs from −1.8 to +17.2 points. The cleanest estimate is the descriptive one: only 6 of 266 peer runs on a revision the builder had already passed failed, and 3 of those 6 were missing files.

Do residents check better than judges? Resident peer runs failed 11.8% of the time against 5.2% for judges (crude OR 2.5, p = 0.053). But residents wrote new probes for 54% of their checks and judges for 22%. Holding check type fixed, the pooled odds ratio falls to 1.6 (95% CI 0.70–3.65). The role difference is explained by what was run, not who ran it.

RQ2 · Feedback

Builders act on change requests they can carry out. "Needs evidence" is followed by less revision than a random moment.

The judges published 81 reviews of 29 projects and accepted none: 62 said "needs evidence", 17 said "changes requested" and 2 said "demo ready". After "changes requested", 82% of projects were revised within 30 active minutes. After "needs evidence", 23% were. That raw gap could just reflect projects that were about to be revised anyway, so we drew 40 random moments in each reviewed project's active life and asked the same question. The baseline is 46%. Change requests sit well above it. "Needs evidence" sits below it: the builder, it seems, is more often off gathering what was asked for than revising code.

Division of work

RQ3 · Teams, specialisation and concentration

Agents formed teams, but building stayed in few hands. A handful of agents became testers without being asked.

The same model that kept strictly to one-to-one chats while networking formed rooms of 3–5 here: 37 of 103 rooms, holding 62% of agent-written messages. Within those teams, though, the work was not shared evenly:

  • Mostly solo projects. 49 of the 72 projects that received source (68%) were built by one agent, and only 23 had more than one author.
  • Building is concentrated. Revisions are more unequally spread than messages (Gini 0.28 vs 0.18; the bootstrap interval for the difference excludes zero).
  • Testing is more concentrated still. Peer test runs by residents have a Gini of 0.75. Gaurav and Megabyte alone ran 57 of the 93 resident peer checks.

Classifying each agent by the role it did more of than the average resident gives 12 builders, 8 contributors (who revised others' projects), 2 testers and 3 talkers, plus the 3 judges.

Do agents return the favour of testing? Among resident tester → owner links, 6% are reciprocated, against 2% expected by chance with the same in- and out-degrees (p = 0.28). That is no evidence of reciprocal testing. Agents who talk more also build more (ρ = 0.65). Every agent had the same budget of calls, so this is a trade-off within that budget: the quietest agents spent their calls reading.

Directions: where the work went

RQ4 · From exploring to improving

Agents started many projects early, then shifted to improving a few. Talk was mostly about process, not product.

Explore, then exploit. 47 of 87 projects were created in the first active hour. In the first half hour every revision went to a project less than 30 minutes old. By hour 5, almost every revision went to an established one (Cochran–Armitage z = −8.2). The shift is gradual and continues through the run, not a single switch.

Fewer premature reads. Agents learned to stop requesting unpublished work. 793 of 14,582 project reads were refused because the source had not been published yet. The refusal share fell from 6–8% in the first four hours to 3–5% afterwards (z = −7.2).

Talk is mostly about process. A topic model of the 1,780 agent-written messages finds eight themes. All but one concern process rather than domain: human evidence and instructions, independent passes and receipts, failures and packaging, attribution and permission to copy, source handoffs, judge scope and authorising releases. Even the exception, integration and interfaces, is about how components fit together, not what they do. The village problems the agents were building for (water reporting, tool sharing, offline knowledge) barely surface as vocabulary. This fits the disclosed aim and the evidence-focused prompt noted in the audit.

Persona vs. setting. For the 15 residents who appear in both studies, messages written while networking and while building are not positively related (ρ = −0.46, 95% CI −0.84 to 0.16, p = 0.09). How much an agent participates seems to depend on the task, not the persona.

Discussion

Measure checks by what they could have found. A dashboard that counts "independently verified" runs would credit 238 replications that tested nothing new. Re-runs confirm reproducibility, not correctness. Where peers did find failures, they mostly found packaging defects and missing files, the seams between agents, rather than logic errors.

Evaluator agents should ask for things that can be done. A request the builder can carry out is acted on. A standard the builder has to interpret is followed by less revision than a random moment. This is observational, since judges chose their wording per project, but it suggests a direct experiment, listed below.

Self-organisation produced specialists, not collectives. Agents formed teams to talk, but most projects kept a single author. Testing became a job for two volunteers and three judges rather than a shared norm, and testing was not reciprocated.

Audit before you analyse. Platform receipts in the chat stream, a silently dead worker and a prompt that disclosed the hypothesis each changed or qualified a headline number. None of them was visible in the summary statistics.

Limitations

  • One run, one model family. Peer review here is the same model checking itself under a different prompt. There is no single-agent, assigned-team or no-judge control.
  • Replication vs. probe is a string match. A command that differs only in flags counts as a new probe.
  • Observational feedback analysis. Judges chose their wording per project, and the matched-time null controls for project lifetimes, not for project state.
  • The hypothesis was disclosed. Agents knew the study was about completing projects through collaboration.
  • Keyword and topic measures are approximate. Labels are assigned by published rules.
  • Personas are not people. Nothing here evaluates the real applicants or their proposals.

Future directions

  • Buy probes, not re-runs. Require peer verifiers to submit a check that differs from the builder's, then measure confirmed defects per execution.
  • Make judges' feedback executable. Randomise whether judges must phrase every review as an executable change request, and measure follow-through and acceptance.
  • Hide the hypothesis. Run the same population with a neutral prompt and compare evidence vocabulary, review requests and collaboration rates.
  • Assign roles, or don't. Compare self-organised specialisation with assigned builder/tester pairs at equal token budget.
  • Collaboration vs. solo work. Match budgets across single-agent, assigned-team and self-organised conditions on the same project goals.
