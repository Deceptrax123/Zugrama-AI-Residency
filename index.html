Zugrama Residency

How agents found teammates, divided work, and turned ideas into tested components

Srinitish Srinivasan

Agent Network Sandbox · November 2026

Read the project cases Zugrama Networking Event

Who built, and who ran the work?

Most entries with source had one author. Many also had an execution by a different agent.

Abstract

How do agents with different project goals find a reason to work together? In Zugrama Residency, 25 GPT-6.1 Sol resident agents entered a shared workshop with goals and skill summaries drawn from residency applications. Three judge agents evaluated the work. The residents chose their own partners and responsibilities over 7.6 active hours.

We analysed 1,780 agent-written messages, 209 source revisions, 577 sandboxed executions and 81 reviews. Rooms with three to five active speakers held 62% of messages. Of 72 project entries with source, 23 had multiple authors. Another 33 had one author and an execution by someone outside the author group. Fifteen agents revised a project someone else created, and two residents accounted for 57 of the 93 resident peer executions.

In the logs, teams formed around overlapping problems, reciprocal offers and components someone else could use. Agents combined two civic-reporting ideas around one workflow, narrowed a sign-language concept to a reusable text adapter, and negotiated a file-by-file handoff for a memory approval tool. The first two produced tested components. The third reached an explicit agreement with the combined implementation still pending at cutoff. These outcomes distinguish an agreed division of work from a delivered and tested solution.

Research questions

  1. Team formation.Which shared goals or complementary needs brought agents together?
  2. Solution discovery.How did they narrow broad project ideas into components that someone could build or use?
  3. Task assignment.Who proposed responsibilities, who accepted them, and which commitments reached a source revision or execution?
  4. Division of work.How widely were implementation and testing distributed across agents and projects?

Team formation

Residents brought individual project goals into a workshop that also offered three village tasks: reporting water, waste and maintenance issues, coordinating shared tools or volunteer time, and retrieving local knowledge offline. A concrete overlap gave agents a reason to open a room and ask for a contribution. The civic team, for example, began when Ganesh saw that two proposals covered the same reporting workflow and asked their authors to build one small slice together.

Group conversation was common. Of 103 rooms, 37 had three to five agents who wrote messages. Those rooms held 1,101 of 1,780 agent-written messages. The network below shows where agents exchanged ideas and where judges and testers connected work across groups.

The Zugrama Networking Event reached 202 of 210 possible pairs and stayed in two-person rooms. Here, agents used larger rooms to coordinate specific deliverables. The studies also differed in scheduling, roles and available tools, so the comparison describes the two settings.

Reassignment · Acceptance · Project record

Division of work

Forty-nine of the 72 project entries with source had one author. On 33 of those 49, another agent ran the code. Across the full set, 15 agents contributed 35 revisions to entries they had not created. The workshop also recorded 24 explicit source handoffs across 20 entries, each announcing a specific revision to a room.

Testing was especially concentrated. Gaurav and Megabyte ran 57 of the 93 resident peer checks, while the three judges ran another 251 peer checks. Resident peer testing had a Gini coefficient of 0.75, compared with 0.28 for source revisions and 0.18 for messages. The activity profiles below show how building, contributing and testing clustered across residents.

How the work changed over time

From new entries to revisions. Agents created 47 of 87 entries in the first active hour. Every first-half-hour revision went to an entry less than 30 minutes old. By hour five, almost every revision went to an older entry (Cochran–Armitage z = −8.2).

Fewer premature reads. Requests reached unpublished work less often as the run progressed. 793 of 14,582 project reads were refused because the source had not been published yet. The refusal share fell from 6–8% in the first four hours to 3–5% afterwards (z = −7.2).

Coordination dominated the chat. The message topic model surfaced recurring language about interfaces, permissions, handoffs, receipts and review. The cases above show what that language did in particular projects: agents set file boundaries, requested a version they could execute, and agreed on which evidence would close the task.

For the 15 residents observed in both studies, the association between message counts was imprecise (Spearman ρ = −0.46, 95% CI −0.84 to 0.16). The two settings offered different tasks and opportunities to contribute.

Testing, feedback and revision

Peer testing

Changing the check surfaced more failures in the raw counts. The difference was uncertain across projects.

We labelled each of the 577 executions by who ran it and what it ran. Builders’ own runs failed 18.9% of the time. Peer replications fail 3.8% of the time and peer new probes 14.2%. Fisher's test on these counts is significant (OR 4.2). But runs are not independent: the same project is checked many times. When we resample whole projects, the 95% interval for the difference runs from −1.8 to +17.2 points. Of 266 peer runs on a revision the builder had already passed, 6 failed. Three of those failures were missing files.

Do residents check better than judges? Resident peer runs failed 11.8% of the time against 5.2% for judges (crude OR 2.5, p = 0.053). But residents wrote new probes for 54% of their checks and judges for 22%. After stratifying by check type, the pooled odds ratio was 1.6 (95% CI 0.70–3.65).

Judge feedback

A revision was more likely to follow a “changes requested” review than a “needs evidence” review.

The judges published 81 reviews of 29 projects and accepted none: 62 said "needs evidence", 17 said "changes requested" and 2 said "demo ready". A revision followed 14 of 17 “changes requested” reviews (82%) and 14 of 62 “needs evidence” reviews (23%) within 30 active minutes. That raw gap could just reflect projects that were about to be revised anyway, so we drew 40 random moments in each reviewed project's active life and asked the same question. The baseline is 46%. Change requests sit well above it. “Needs evidence” sits below it. Judges chose the review wording, and we did not measure how builders spent the intervening time.

Discussion

Teams formed around a specific need. The civic team reduced duplicate work by agreeing on one common workflow. The record-store pair traded implementation for review. The text adapter worked across projects because the supplier defined a usable boundary and the recipient tested it after copying.

Much of the division of work was between authors and reviewers. A project could have one source author and still receive useful outside testing. Conversely, the memory approval team named owners for all remaining files and supplied acceptance tests, yet never recorded an execution of the combined version. An assessment of collaboration therefore needs the assignment, the artifact it produced, and the scope of the test.

Together with the Zugrama Networking Event, these records show how the same model could use conversation for different purposes. Networking made it possible to find partners and develop ideas across topics. The workshop made more of the work visible through specific responsibilities, versioned handoffs and execution records.

Methods

Population

25 residents + 3 judges

Residents were seeded with stated goals, sourced skill summaries and proposed acceptance checks. The judges had fixed remits: Lou (village usefulness), Danish (technical correctness and privacy) and Guru (delivery and scope). All agents ran GPT-6.1 Sol with high reasoning.

Workshop

Versioned code + sandbox

Agents could create, write, run, hand off and share projects. Runs execute in a network-isolated sandbox and return receipts bound to source hashes. A round-robin scheduler gave each agent about 275 model calls.

Clock

7.6 active hours

All elapsed times use the active clock.

Inclusion
We include agent-written messages only. Of 2,731 messages in the record, 951 were posted by the platform under an agent's name: 433 execution receipts, 311 revision notices, 71 review notices and 136 workspace or whiteboard publications. All executions, revisions and reviews are included.
Measures
  • Room size counts distinct speakers with an agent-written message. Co-authorship counts distinct authors of committed source revisions on the same entry.
  • Outside execution is a sandboxed run by an agent outside the set of everyone who authored source for that entry by cutoff. This is a stricter measure than the non-creator runs used in the verification tests.
  • Source handoff counts successful explicit handoff calls for a specific revision. Task agreements and cross-entry reuse are traced from messages and artifacts in selected cases.
  • Self run: an execution by the project's creator.
  • Peer replication: a non-creator runs a command string already run on that project.
  • Peer new probe: a non-creator runs a command never run on that project before.
  • Failure: a non-zero exit or ok: false.
  • Acted on: a new revision of the project within 30 active minutes of a review.
  • Role profile: each agent's counts of own-project revisions, revisions to others' projects, self runs, peer runs, messages and reviews. The agent's archetype is the role whose share most exceeds the resident average.
Statistics
  • Wilson intervals for proportions.
  • Fisher's exact test with Woolf intervals on odds ratios.
  • A cluster bootstrap over projects (3,000 resamples) for verification differences.
  • A Mantel–Haenszel pooled odds ratio stratified by check type.
  • A matched-time null for review follow-through: 40 random time points per reviewed project, drawn within that project's active life.
  • A directed configuration null (2,000 draws) for reciprocal testing.
  • An agent-level bootstrap for Gini coefficients and Spearman correlations.
  • Cochran–Armitage tests for trends over active hours.
Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across the 8 tests that produce a p-value.

Run audit

We checked whether the run happened as designed by comparing four sources: the scheduler (when each agent was called), the raw model requests and responses, the usage ledger and the platform's records of messages, tools and projects.

Corrects earlier numbersOur first draft and the earlier automated audit counted all 2,731 chat messages as agent talk. Excluding the 951 platform receipts changes several results:
  • Median words per message: 47 → 55.
  • Judges' message counts: 171–204 → 116–142.
  • Talk–build correlation: 0.79 → 0.65.
  • Cross-setting correlation: −0.36 → −0.46.

All numbers on this page use the corrected record.

Demand characteristics. Every system prompt began: It also told agents to "distinguish a proposed approach, a draft artifact, a tested artifact and externally validated completion" and to "preserve failures". The evidence-heavy vocabulary we observe (receipts, scopes, "not human adoption evidence") is at least partly an instruction-following effect.

Limitations

  • One run, one model family. Peer review here is the same model checking itself under a different prompt. There is no single-agent, assigned-team or no-judge control.
  • Replication vs. probe is a string match. A command that differs only in flags counts as a new probe.
  • Observational feedback analysis. Judges chose their wording per project, and the matched-time null controls for project lifetimes, not for project state.
  • The hypothesis was disclosed. Agents knew the study was about completing projects through collaboration.
  • Keyword and topic measures are approximate. Labels are assigned by published rules.
  • Selected cases. The project paths illustrate how work was negotiated. They do not estimate how often assignments were accepted, and code authorship misses work done in separate entries or in chat.
  • Personas are not people. Nothing here evaluates the real applicants or their proposals.

Future directions

  • Buy probes, not re-runs. Require peer verifiers to submit a check that differs from the builder's, then measure confirmed defects per execution.
  • Make judges' feedback executable. Randomise whether judges must phrase every review as an executable change request, and measure follow-through and acceptance.
  • Hide the hypothesis. Run the same population with a neutral prompt and compare evidence vocabulary, review requests and collaboration rates.
  • Assign roles, or don't. Compare self-organised specialisation with assigned builder/tester pairs at equal token budget.
  • Collaboration vs. solo work. Match budgets across single-agent, assigned-team and self-organised conditions on the same project goals.
